# A Negotiator Reads Your Selling Page

**Author:** Chris Voss (as requested) · **Subject:** `/freelance` landing page · **Date:** 2026-11-15

---

I spent 24 years in the FBI talking people out of rooms they didn't want to leave.
Your visitor just spent eight seconds deciding whether *you* are safe to talk to.
That's a hostage negotiation with worse stakes — they can hang up on you forever with one click.

I read the whole page. Twice — the second time as the buyer, because the buyer is the
only hostage here. Here is my debrief.

---

## What you already got right (I build rapport first, even in a critique)

- **"Handoff, not hostage."** That's the strongest line on the page. I've used that exact
  frame in kidnappings: name the buyer's worst fear of the *ending* and promise the other
  ending. Whoever wrote that line understands loss aversion, even if nobody ever named it.
- **"Fixed proposal. No hourly surprises."** You refused the F-word — "fair" — and anchored
  on structure instead of price. Good. Amateurs sell hours; professionals sell outcomes
  with a fence around them.
- **"Scope call. You leave with a written scope — even if we don't work together."**
  That's a unilateral concession made *before* any commitment. In the Bureau we called
  this giving a gift. It disarms. Keep it, and mean it.
- **Weekly demos.** Continuous small "that's right" moments. Every demo is the buyer
  saying *that's right, this is working* — and "that's right" is the sound of trust
  being manufactured.
- **The Commercial engagements band.** YourClient doesn't pick vendors off a leaderboard.
  Putting the vetting institutions next to the products is institutional proof —
  the closest a web page gets to a hostage negotiator saying "the people who sent me
  have already checked your files."

Now. Where you're losing the negotiation.

---

## Miss #1 — You never ran the Accusation Audit

This is the whole game, and the page skips it.

Your buyer is not thinking "I hope this man knows LangGraph." Your buyer is thinking:

- *The last freelancer ghosted at 70%.*
- *The one before that billed hours I couldn't trace.*
- *This one will demo something beautiful and disappear when the hard part starts.*

Every buyer arrives pre-loaded with accusations against you. **The first side to say
them out loud wins.** Your page never says them. It walks in confident, smiling, with
features — while the buyer sits on a pile of unsaid suspicions.

Fix — put this directly under the hero, before any service:

> You've hired freelancers before. Maybe one vanished mid-sprint. Maybe another billed
> hours you couldn't trace, or shipped something that worked in the demo and fell over
> on Monday. You're not shopping for code. You're shopping for the version of this
> story that ends differently.

That paragraph does more work than five service cards. Label their fear before they
can weaponize it, and you've taken the gun off the table.

---

## Miss #2 — The page sells you, not their situation

Count the "I"s. "I'm Your Name… I build… I deliver… I take retainers." Tactical empathy
isn't about you being trustworthy — it's about *demonstrating you understand their world*
better than they can articulate it themselves.

Right now the hero says what you are. It never mirrors what they're in. Mirroring on a
page means echoing the buyer's situation in their own words:

> Legacy RAG that hallucinates in production? A dashboard nobody trusts? A deadline
> your last developer walked away from?

One line. Late-night FM DJ voice — calm, downward-inflecting, no exclamation marks.
You can't mirror what you refuse to look at.

---

## Miss #3 — No calibrated question anywhere

"How" and "What" questions make the other side solve your problem while feeling in
control. Your page asks zero. It declares, then demands commitment ("Start a project")
— that's a command, and commands trigger the "No" reflex in people who've been burned.

Recalibrate. Put one question in the contact section:

> **What does "done" look like for you — and what does it cost you if it ships late?**

That second half is loss aversion doing push-ups. Buyers take risks to avoid losses
far more readily than to capture gains. Your headline is a gain ("shipped end to end").
You never price the alternative — the cost of *not* shipping, the quarter lost to a
dead pipeline, the compliance fine from an AI feature nobody evaluated. The most
expensive sentence on your page is the one you didn't write about doing nothing.

---

## Miss #4 — Your pricing answer is an evasion

> *"It depends on scope."*

Every buyer reads "it depends" as *"I'm hiding something."* You follow it with "fixed
price per milestone" — good — but you never anchor. In every negotiation, somebody
anchors; if you don't, the buyer anchors at zero and you negotiate upward from insult.

You don't need exact numbers. You need a range, stated with a calm voice:

> Most fixed-scope engagements land between the cost of a used car and the cost of a
> junior hire's first year — decided in one call, before you owe me anything.

Or simply: "Engagements typically run $Xk–$Yk per milestone." A range is an anchor
and a filter at the same time. It makes the wrong clients say "no" politely — and "no"
early is cheap; "no" late is expensive.

---

## Miss #5 — You buried your Black Swan

Here's what nobody visiting that page will ever learn: you spent a **decade teaching
and running a school** before you ever shipped LLM systems. You cut churn by half with
*no software at all* — by listening to people who were scared and frustrated.

That's not a fun fact. That's a Black Swan. It's the reason a non-technical founder
should trust you with their product: you've already done the hardest version of this
job — explaining systems to scared humans — for ten years, with no demo to hide behind.

The freelance buyer's deepest fear is a brilliant engineer who can't communicate.
Your history is the antidote, and the page never mentions it. Put it somewhere:

> Before engineering, I spent ten years teaching and running a school. Freelancing
> with me means the person explaining your system speaks human first.

---

## Miss #6 — Your AI card speaks Bureau-ese

"Context engineering. Harness engineering. Agentic SDLC flows. EvalGates."

I negotiated with terrorists and *I* had to re-read that list. If the buyer can't
repeat your value in their own words, you haven't persuaded them — you've lectured
them. Translate one notch:

- "Context engineering" → *Your AI stops hallucinating because I control exactly what it reads.*
- "Quality gates" → *Nothing ships if it violates the architecture — the gates fire before I call it done.*
- "Agentic SDLC" → *The boring parts of development run themselves; you pay for judgment, not typing.*

Same substance. Their language. He who frames the problem in the buyer's words owns
the solution.

---

## Miss #7 — "Start a project" asks for marriage on the first date

Your primary CTA is an email that promises… a conversation. The buyer hears: *now I
have to compose a good project brief, state my budget, expose my ignorance.* That's
a commitment. Commitments trigger "No."

Give them a smaller first "no":

> **"Would it be ridiculous to get a fixed-scope plan before you commit to anything?"**

Rename the email CTA: *"Get a fixed-scope plan"* — now the email promises a deliverable,
not a conversation. And keep "Schedule a call" prominent — voice carries 38% of the
message, and Calendly is the closest a web page gets to your tone of voice. You have
it, but it's the *secondary* button. For a freelance page, voice is the product.
Flip them.

## The Negotiator's Scorecard

| Section | Lens | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Hero headline | Gain framing | Clean, but no loss framing anywhere — add the cost of inaction |
| Availability badge | Trust signal | Good — scarcity with a pulse |
| "Trusted by YourClient…" | Institutional proof | Strong; say *out loud* that they vetted you |
| Proof strip (hours → seconds, 8 teams) | Logic proof | Solid numbers, zero emotion — attach the founder story |
| Services | Feature density | Jargon-heavy; translate to buyer outcomes |
| "Handoff, not hostage" | Loss aversion | **Best line on the page.** Build the section headline around it |
| Process | "That's right" engine | Weekly demos = engineered trust; keep |
| FAQ pricing | Anchoring | "It depends" reads as evasion — anchor with a range |
| CTA "Start a project" | Commitment ladder | Too big a first ask — offer the plan, not the marriage |
| FAQ | Objection labels | Right idea; rewrite as No-oriented questions |
| Overall | Accusation audit | **Missing entirely.** This is the one |

## If I had to leave you with three moves

1. **Run the Accusation Audit above the fold's shadow.** Name the ghost, the bill, the
   Monday crash — before the buyer names them to themselves. The first one to say it
   owns it.
2. **Anchor, then ladder.** A price range in the FAQ (or "cost of a junior hire's
   first year" if numbers feel early), an email CTA that promises a *fixed-scope plan*,
   and Calendly promoted to primary. Small "no" first, voice second, contract last.
3. **Unbury the Black Swan.** Ten years teaching is why the scary parts of this job —
   the scared stakeholders, the moving requirements — are the parts you're calmest in.
   That story is worth more than the fourth proof card. And it's true, which in my
   business is the only kind of leverage that survives the close.

You don't need more proof. You need the buyer to hear, in the first scroll, that you
already know what went wrong last time — and that you've built the page so it can't
happen again. Get to "that's right," and the budget finds itself.

**Trust me.**
